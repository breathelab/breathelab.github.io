Laboratory of Bioengineered Respiratory Emulation Approaches for Tuberculosis and Health

We develop cutting-edge human in vitro lung models using human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSC) to understand how Mycobacterium tuberculosis interacts with the lung innate immunity, and what determines the success or failure of anitmicrobial responses and tissue homeostasis.

Bacteria

Development of Mycobacterium tuberculosis reporter systems to reveal the heterogeonous bacterial states that overcomes host early innate immune responses.

Cell

Differentiation of alveolar epithelium, macrophages, endothelium and disease-relevant cell types from genetically defined iPSC to establish host genetic requirements.

Device

Eningeering of fit-for-purpose microfluidic organ-on-chip system to emulate the distinct pathophysiological and microenvironmental features of TB.

Patients

Translation of lab-generated knowledge to clinincal TB patients in East London, and incorporation of key clinical observations to develop disease-inspired in vitro models.

Research

Our work sits at the interface of infection microbiology and bioengineering. We believe the development of physiologically relevant and fit for purpose complex in vitro lung models will enable us to better emulate and understand the host-TB interactions and TB pathogenesis.

1. Alveolus-on-Chip models for early host-TB interactions

Aerosol droplets carring M. tuberculosis land in the alveolus, the bacteria are captured by alveolar macrophages that serve as the earliest gatekeeper of whether the bacterial invasion is successful. M. tuberculosis has evolved a sophisicated arsenal of pathogenic effectors to evade the host innate and adaptive immunity, yet only a fraction of exposed individuals developed Tuberculosis. Tuberculosis has an asymtopmatic phase of months, which makes the earliest phases of the infection clinically inaccessible, while animal models have limitation in recapitulating the pathogenesis of a human pathogen. To address the technological and scientific gaps, we apply iPSC-derived alveolus-on-chip models to investigate the early host-M. tuberculosis interactions that determine the success of bacterial invasion and niche formation.
